Most advice about jobs and AI starts with a prediction. This site starts somewhere more useful: with the parts of hiring you can look at today and reason about yourself.

An employer hiring someone is trying to answer one question. Can this person do the work, and will it go well if we bring them in? AI has changed how easy it is to look like the answer is yes. It has not changed the question.

## What changed

**Applications got cheap.** A tailored résumé and a cover letter used to take an evening. Now they take minutes, for anyone. When something gets cheap to produce, it stops carrying much information. A polished cover letter once suggested effort and care. It now suggests very little.

**Screening got more automated, on both sides.** Candidates use tools to apply and employers use tools to sort. That can make the front door feel like two machines talking to each other, and sending more applications into it is a tempting and usually poor response.

**Some tasks got faster.** First drafts, boilerplate code, summaries and routine analysis take less time than they did. Work built only from those tasks is the most exposed. Work that uses them as a step toward something harder has often become more productive.

**Employers started testing differently.** Take-home tasks are harder to interpret when a model can complete them, so many hiring teams are rethinking them. Expect more conversation about how you worked, and more questions about your tools.

## What didn't change

**Trust still moves through people.** A referral from someone who has seen your work tells an employer something no generated document can. It was valuable before, and it is worth more now that documents tell them less.

**Evidence still beats claims.** "Detail-oriented self-starter" was weak before AI, and it is weaker now. Something real that you made, shipped or fixed, and can talk through in detail, remains hard to fake.

**Judgement is still what gets paid for.** Knowing which problem matters, noticing when an answer is wrong and taking responsibility for the result are what a tool does not do for you. Someone still has to own the outcome.

**A conversation is still hard to fake.** If you did the work, you can answer the fifth follow-up question about it. If you didn't, you usually can't.

## What to do with this

If documents carry less signal, move your effort to the things that still carry it:

1. **Apply to fewer places, better.** Spend the time you would have spent on the next twenty applications finding out who works at the three you care about most.
2. **Make your work visible.** One real project you can explain beats ten bullet points you can't.
3. **Talk to people.** Not to extract a referral, but to learn what the job is really like. Referrals tend to follow from that.
4. **Use the tools, and be able to say how.** Being fluent with AI is increasingly assumed for many roles. Being able to describe where you trust it and where you don't is the part that stands out.

Nobody can honestly tell you what the job market will look like in five years, and you should be careful with anyone who claims to. What you can do is build what has always been worth having and what is still hard to imitate: real skill, real evidence and real relationships.
