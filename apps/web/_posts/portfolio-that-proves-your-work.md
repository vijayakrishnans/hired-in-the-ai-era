When anyone can generate a polished résumé, it gets harder for a reader to tell what is real. A portfolio helps with that, but only if it is built to show the thing that can't be generated: how you think.

A gallery of finished work doesn't do this any more. A finished result, on its own, doesn't tell the reader who or what produced it. The process around it does.

## This works outside software too

"Portfolio" sounds like something for developers and designers. It isn't. A marketer can write up a campaign and what it taught them. An operations manager can describe a process they fixed. A teacher can show a lesson they redesigned and why. Anyone can write about a problem they solved, as long as they keep confidential details out of it.

## What to put in it

**A few pieces, not many.** Two or three that you can talk about for half an hour are better than a dozen you can't. A reader will look at very little, so choose what they see.

**Real problems over exercises.** Something that someone actually uses, even if it's only a few people, teaches you things no tutorial can. It also gives you the most convincing kind of story: what happened when it met reality.

**The write-up, for each piece:**

- **The problem.** What was wrong, and for whom?
- **The options.** What did you consider, and why did you reject the others?
- **The decision.** What did you do, and what did you give up to do it?
- **The result.** What actually happened? Use real measurements if you have them, and be plain about it if you don't.
- **What you'd change.** This is often the most convincing section, because it's the one a person who wasn't really there can't write.

## Show your failures

A project that went wrong and that you can explain clearly can say more about you than a success. A reader learns how you notice problems, how you respond to them and whether you tell the truth about them.

## Be open about how you used AI

If you used AI tools, say so, and say how. "I used an assistant for the first draft of the data-cleaning script, then found it was silently dropping rows with missing dates, so I rewrote that part and added a test" tells the reader three good things about you at once. It also means you never have to wonder what happens if somebody asks.

## Make it easy to read

Whoever opens your portfolio is probably short on time.

- Lead with the result, and put the detail below it.
- Use a plain page. A simple site or a well-written document is fine. The work is what's being judged.
- Check that every link works and that it reads well on a phone.
- Don't publish anything belonging to an employer or client without permission. Describe the shape of the problem instead, and say that you've done so.

## The test

Before you send it to anyone, ask a friend to read one project, then ask you the hardest question they can think of. If you can answer it, and enjoy answering it, then the piece is doing its job. If you can't, you've found out what to work on next.
