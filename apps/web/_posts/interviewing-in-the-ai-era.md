Interviews are in transition. Some employers ban AI in assessments, some expect you to use it, and some haven't decided. What they're all trying to find out is the same as it ever was: how you think, and what it would be like to work with you.

## First, find out the rules

Before any assessment, ask. A simple question does it: "Are AI tools allowed for this, and is there anything you'd like me to do or avoid?"

This isn't a trap, and it won't count against you. Hiring teams often welcome it because it shows you take the question seriously. If the answer is no, then it's no, for the whole task. Being found to have broken that rule will usually end the process, and it should.

## Take-home tasks

If tools are allowed, assume whoever reads your work knows that they can produce a passable answer. Passable is no longer what sets you apart. So:

- **Understand everything you hand in.** You should be able to explain any line or any sentence, because you may be asked to.
- **Write down your decisions.** A short note on what you chose, what you skipped and what you'd do with more time can be the strongest part of the submission.
- **Say how you used tools.** One or two honest sentences are enough.
- **Respect the time limit.** Stopping when asked and saying what's left shows good judgement. It's also a fair way to treat a task you aren't being paid for.

## Live exercises

Whether it's coding, a case study or a working session, the point is to watch you reason.

- **Think aloud.** Silence gives the interviewer nothing to assess. Say what you're considering, even if you're unsure.
- **Clarify first.** Ask what matters and what you can assume. It's what you'd do in the real job.
- **Say when you don't know.** "I don't remember the syntax, here's what I'd look up and why" is a fine answer.
- **If tools are allowed, use them as you would at work.** Say what you're asking for, read what comes back and check it out loud. Catching a mistake in a tool's output, in front of someone, is a very good moment.

## Questions about how you work with AI

Expect these, and prepare a real example:

- When did a tool save you real time?
- When did it give you something wrong, and how did you notice?
- What wouldn't you use it for?
- How do you check its work?

Avoid both easy extremes. "I use it for everything" suggests you may not check. "I never touch it" can suggest you haven't looked, unless you can explain why it doesn't fit your work. A specific story with a specific lesson beats either.

## How to practise

1. **Prepare a few stories in detail.** Pick work you know well, and be ready for the fifth "why?". Being able to go that deep is good evidence that you did it.
2. **Rehearse aloud.** Have a tool or a friend play a sceptical interviewer. Answers you've only rehearsed in your head tend to fall apart on first contact.
3. **Don't memorise generated answers.** They sound memorised, and they collapse under a follow-up question.

## Using AI during a live interview

Don't use hidden tools to feed you answers in a live interview. Quite apart from being dishonest, it's a bad bet: if it works, you've got a job you may not be able to do, and if it's noticed, you've lost the offer and possibly your reputation with people you might meet again.

## Ask them too

An interview goes both ways. Ask how the team actually uses these tools, who's responsible when an automated output is wrong, and how they've changed the way they develop junior people. The answers will tell you a lot about what it would be like to work there.
