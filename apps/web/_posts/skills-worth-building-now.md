Lists of "future-proof skills" tend to age badly, and the tools change too quickly to bet everything on any one of them. A more durable way to choose is to ask which skills get *more* valuable as the tools get better.

Three pass that test.

## Judgement

A model can produce ten options in a minute. Somebody has to know which one is right, which two are subtly wrong and whether the question was the right question at all. The cheaper it gets to generate things, the more the bottleneck moves to evaluating them.

Judgement can feel too vague to practise, but it can be trained:

- **Make predictions and check them.** Before you run the test, launch the campaign or send the proposal, write down what you expect to happen. Then compare. It is one of the fastest feedback loops there is.
- **Review other people's work.** Code review, editing and critique force you to say what is wrong and why.
- **Keep a record of your decisions.** Note what you chose, what you rejected and why. You will learn more from reading it six months later than you expect.

## Domain knowledge

General AI tools are widely available, so general skill with them doesn't set you apart for long. What does is knowing a field well enough to spot the error that looks fine to everyone else.

A nurse who understands AI tools, an accountant who can automate their own reconciliation or a logistics planner who can build a small forecasting script are each hard to replace. They know what the output should look like, and they know what the real constraints are.

This is good news for career changers. Your previous industry is not wasted time. It may be the most valuable thing you bring, because plenty of people know the tools and far fewer know the tools *and* how a hospital ward or a warehouse actually runs.

## Working with AI

This is a practical skill, and you build it the way you build any other: on real work rather than on tutorials.

- **Learn where it fails in your field.** Use it on a problem you already know the answer to, and see where it goes wrong. It will likely be confident and wrong in patterns you can learn to spot.
- **Learn to verify cheaply.** Know how to check an answer without redoing all of the work: run the test, follow the citation, try the edge case.
- **Learn to break work down.** Tools do better on a well-specified piece than on a vague whole, and specifying the work is itself the hard, valuable part.

## The skills underneath

A few older skills matter more, not less, when output gets cheap. Clear writing, because a clear brief gets a better result from people and tools alike. Explaining your reasoning to someone who disagrees. Asking a good question. Finishing things.

## Choosing what to learn next

When you are deciding what to work on, try these three questions:

1. Would this still be useful if the current tools were twice as good?
2. Can I practise it on real work, this month?
3. Would somebody in the job I want notice if I were good at it?

If you can't answer the third, go and ask someone who does that job. It is a better guide than any list of skills, including this one.
